A mock test is more than a practice paper. It shows you how you perform under a clock, where you lose marks, and what to study next. Used well, each mock becomes a study plan for the following week. Used badly, it is just a number that makes you anxious.

## Why mocks matter

Chapter-wise practice teaches you the material. A full mock tests something different:

- **Speed:** can you finish the paper in the time allowed?
- **Stamina:** can you stay focused from the first question to the last?
- **Decisions:** do you know when to skip, when to guess and when to come back?
- **Recognition:** can you spot the topic of a question without a chapter heading above it?

You can only build these skills by sitting full papers under real conditions.

## When to start taking mocks

Take one baseline mock early, even before you feel ready. Its job is not to give you a good score. Its job is to show you where you stand, so you know which chapters need the most time.

After that, keep the rhythm simple:

- **Early preparation:** mostly chapter-wise practice, with an occasional full mock to check progress.
- **Middle stretch:** about one full mock a week, reviewed properly.
- **Final weeks:** a mock every two or three days, with fixing weak chapters in between.
- **Last two days:** no new mocks. Revise your notes and rest.

## Simulate real exam conditions

A mock only predicts test day if it feels like test day.

- Sit at the same time of day as your real test, if you know it.
- Do the whole paper in one sitting, with no pauses.
- Keep your phone in another room. No notes, no textbook, no searching.
- Use only what you will have in the hall, such as rough paper, and follow the official rules on calculators.
- Apply the real marking scheme. If your test has negative marking, count it.

## Pacing: know your time per question

Work out your average time per question before you start. Two examples from the criteria guide our merit calculator uses:

- **NUST NET (Engineering and Computing):** 200 MCQs in 3 hours, about 54 seconds per question.
- **ECAT:** 100 MCQs in 100 minutes, one minute per question.

Test formats can change between sessions, so confirm the current format in the official notification for your test.

An average is not a rule for every question. Some will take ten seconds, others two minutes. A two-pass approach helps:

1. **First pass:** answer everything you can solve quickly and confidently. Mark the rest.
2. **Second pass:** return to the marked questions with the time you saved.
3. **Final minutes:** decide on any remaining blanks based on your test's marking scheme.

If your test has negative marking, read our [ECAT strategy guide](/blog/ecat-preparation-strategy-uet-lahore) for a simple way to decide when a guess is worth it.

## Review every mock, properly

The review is where the real learning happens. Plan to spend about as long reviewing a mock as you spent taking it.

### Sort your mistakes

Put every wrong or guessed answer into one of these groups:

- **Concept gap:** you did not know the idea. Go back to the chapter.
- **Careless slip:** you knew it but misread the question or made an arithmetic error. Slow down on that question type.
- **Time pressure:** you rushed or never reached it. Work on pacing.
- **Lucky guess:** you got it right but were not sure. Treat it as a mistake.

### Keep a mistake log

Write a one-line note for each mistake: the chapter, what went wrong and the correct idea. Reread the log before each new mock. Over time, you will see the same few chapters appear again and again. Those are your priorities.

### Redo, do not just reread

A few days later, try the questions you got wrong again without looking at the answer. If you can solve them cold, the lesson has stuck.

## Avoid burnout

Mocks are tiring, and more is not always better.

- **Space them out.** A mock without a review is mostly wasted time.
- **Protect your sleep.** Tired practice builds tired habits.
- **Take a real rest day each week.** Your memory needs time to consolidate.
- **Watch the trend, not one score.** A bad mock is information, not a verdict.
- **Talk to someone** if stress starts affecting your sleep or health. Family, friends or a teacher can help you keep perspective.
