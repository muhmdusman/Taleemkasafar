If NUST is your target, the NUST Entry Test (NET) deserves most of your attention. It carries more weight in the NUST aggregate than your FSc and Matric combined. This guide covers what the paper looks like, why the test matters so much, and an 8-week plan you can adapt to your own schedule.

## What the NET looks like

According to the criteria guide our merit calculator is built on, the Engineering and Computing NET has:

- **200 MCQs** in **3 hours**
- **Mathematics:** 80 questions
- **Physics:** 60 questions
- **Chemistry or Computer Science:** 30 questions
- **English:** 20 questions
- **Intelligence:** 10 questions
- **No negative marking**

That works out to about 54 seconds per question. Our source only describes the Engineering and Computing paper, so if you are applying to another NUST programme, check its own test format.

The same source notes that NUST holds more than one NET series in an academic year and counts your highest score. That is good news: one weak attempt does not have to define your result.

## Why the test dominates your aggregate

The NUST formula gives NET 75%, FSc 15% and Matric 10%. NET is marked out of 200, FSc and Matric out of 1100.

Look at what a single mark is worth:

- One NET mark adds 75 ÷ 200 = **0.375** aggregate points.
- One FSc mark adds 15 ÷ 1100 ≈ **0.014** aggregate points.

In other words, one extra NET mark is worth roughly as much as 27 extra FSc marks. By the time you sit NET, your Matric is fixed and your FSc is mostly settled. The test is the part you can still move, and it moves the aggregate a lot.

Mathematics alone is 80 of the 200 questions, so it is the single biggest lever in the paper. Physics comes next at 60.

Our source guide does not state which admission year this formula applies to, and formulas change between sessions. Verify the current criteria with the official NUST admission website before you apply.

## An 8-week NET study plan

Shorten or stretch this plan to fit your schedule. Six weeks works if your FSc concepts are already strong.

### Weeks 1–2: Find your baseline and rebuild basics

- Take one full-length timed mock before you study anything. The score is only a starting point.
- List the chapters where you lost the most marks.
- Rebuild Mathematics and Physics fundamentals from your FSc Part 1 and Part 2 textbooks.
- Read English for 20 minutes a day: vocabulary, grammar rules and short passages.

### Weeks 3–4: Chapter-wise practice

- Practise Mathematics and Physics every day, one or two chapters at a time.
- Alternate Chemistry or Computer Science on the remaining days.
- Read the explanation for every question you get wrong, and for every one you guessed right.
- Keep a short notebook of formulas and mistakes.

### Weeks 5–6: Mixed practice and weekly mocks

- Switch from single-chapter sets to mixed sets, so you learn to recognise a topic without a heading.
- Take one full mock per week under real conditions, then review it the next day.
- Add a few Intelligence questions to your daily routine.

### Weeks 7–8: Exam mode

- Take a full mock every two or three days.
- Spend the days in between fixing the weak chapters each mock exposes.
- Practise pacing: first pass for questions you can solve quickly, second pass for the rest.
- Ease off in the last two days. Revise your notebook, sleep well and stop taking new mocks.

## Subject-wise approach

### Mathematics

Speed comes from fluency, not shortcuts. Drill core FSc topics until standard question types feel routine, then work on timed sets. Since there is no negative marking in the source format, never leave a Mathematics question blank. Make your best attempt and move on.

### Physics

Physics rewards understanding concepts over memorising answers. For each chapter, know the key formulas, their units and the situations where they apply. Numerical questions often hinge on one small idea, so write that idea in your notebook when you miss one.

### Chemistry or Computer Science

With 30 questions, this section is smaller but still worth steady marks. Focus on definitions, standard reactions or core programming concepts from your FSc syllabus, and revise them in short, frequent sessions.

### English and Intelligence

These 30 questions together are often the quickest marks on the paper. Regular reading, a growing vocabulary list and a few pattern-recognition puzzles a day are enough to keep them sharp.
